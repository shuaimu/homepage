Coverage of the original FAST 2021 presentation

Audit date: September 20, 2026.

Source: Siying Dong’s official presentation on USENIX, 48-page slide PDF. The downloaded PDF’s SHA-256 is d0ece2700caf400efe08ea82ba60a9f7c6e34dbbe239dc6daac97b794994c2ba.

All original PDF pages were inspected, including diagram-only pages and repeated reveal frames. This audit covers the posted slides, not additional spoken material in the video. Lecture numbers below are logical slide numbers printed in the footer and match handout pages. The presentation PDF has 77 pages because five slides have an initial question state followed by an answer reveal.

Result

The revised lecture covers every substantive topic, example, and conclusion in the original slides, across 64 core slides and seven appendix content slides, preceded by an appendix divider. Repeated frames are consolidated; the original section dividers and closing branding add no technical content and are not copied. Data integrity and recovery precedes operations in the selected teaching order.

The earlier 40-slide version covered the paper broadly, but several points that the talk treats in detail appeared only as brief mentions. The revision adds:

  • 35: CPU/SSD provisioning imbalance and disaggregated storage.
  • 39: The original host 1 → 4 → 5 corruption-spreading sequence, including retirement of healthy hosts 2 and 3.
  • 41: Persisting an SST’s expected checksum at creation, rejecting bad file copies, and verifying both backup and restore.
  • 43: Per-record verification at write, flush, compaction, and read boundaries.
  • 44: The gap between WAL checksums and reliable storage, and receiver-side verification of a caller-supplied handoff checksum.
  • 69–70: Every milestone in the talk’s 2012–2020 timeline.
  • 71: The original utilization chart, including its measurement definitions.
  • 72: The talk’s amplification estimates, distinguished from the paper’s numbers and the compaction-policy benchmark.

Slides 5, 39, and 47 also make the hardware goals and the multi-host, multi-instance replication setting explicit. The instructor notes explain why early detection permits recovery from a healthy replica, while checksum detection alone cannot reconstruct a correct value.

The opening now also includes LevelDB’s 2011 origins and Facebook’s 2012 fork on slides 2–3. These use the original Google and Facebook announcements linked in the speaker notes and README, supplementing the talk’s project timeline.

The later sections also include explicit definitions and examples for WAL recovery (slide 48), checksum creation and verification (37), independent shard sequence numbers (57), and a worked design discussion (63). These teaching additions expand the explanations while preserving the original talk coverage. Slides 55–56 additionally define who assigns sequence numbers and user timestamps, what each orders, and how both limits constrain a read. The two-version example and per-key timestamp-ordering rule are checked against the RocksDB timestamp and snapshot documentation linked in the speaker notes. They explain the concepts without attributing later API availability to the 2021 paper.

Qualifications preserved

  • The timeline dates and production deployments follow the talk. Its 2017 NVM work is an experiment, and timestamp support in 2020 is partial. The names MongoRocks/MongoDB, MyRocks/MySQL, and Rocksandra/Cassandra are identified.
  • Original pages 9–10 describe engine amplification as often hundreds, filesystem amplification as near one, and device amplification as one to three. Paper §3 instead says software amplification can reach about 100 and SSD amplification was observed at 1.1–3. Slide 72 states this difference; neither set is substituted for the RocksDB 5.9 Table 3 measurements on slide 22.
  • The 42-deployment chart is distinct from Table 2’s four category examples. CPU/read-bandwidth values use the highest hour, while space/endurance use the month average. All are averaged across hosts within each deployment.
  • File checksums use an expectation established when the SST was created. The 2021 whole-file protection did not cover WAL files; the handoff discussion addresses a separate write boundary.
  • Per-KV protection was proposed/in progress in 2021. A handoff check depends on receiver/API support; it is not guaranteed by an ordinary filesystem write.

Teaching route and artifacts

The core route is slides 1–64, with suggested notes totaling 96 minutes. For the 80-minute class, omit the prior-lecture review on slides 6–13 (10 minutes) and the discussion activities on 23 and 62–63 (7 minutes), leaving one minute for transitions. The HDD in-place update, SSD block-copy example, and LSM motivation are on 15–17, after the “Why do we need LSM on SSD?” divider on 14. Each compaction policy has its own example on 19–21, after the divider on 18. The application-policy discussion on 23 lists the suggested policy and reason beside each application, with all answers visible immediately. The Bloomfilter divider and five introductory slides are on 24–29. Title pages also introduce resource optimization (30), data integrity and recovery (36), operations (46), versions and timestamps (54), and discussion and takeaways (61). Appendix slides 66–72, after the divider on 65, remain in both PDFs and add about 12 minutes if all are presented. The detailed 2012–2020 timeline, full survey figure, and rough amplification estimates are therefore available for completeness without making every appendix page mandatory in the core route.

The regenerated lecture and handout retain the selected textbook styling and five question/answer reveals. All new conceptual figures are editable CeTZ. The utilization chart is an attributed source image, not reconstructed data; extraction provenance is in assets/README.md.
